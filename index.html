Search settings

A run starts from the 25-cube product construction stretched to side s₀ and adds n − 25 cubes at random spots. It then minimizes the box side under no-overlap constraints. The seed fixes the random choices, so the same seed and settings give the same run. Different browsers can round slightly differently and drift apart late in a run. With the defaults, seed 23 settles near 2.9435 for n = 26. Basin hop starts from whatever is on screen.

The problem

How small can a d-dimensional cube be if it must hold n unit d-cubes without overlap? The cubes may be rotated freely. In two and three dimensions this is a long-running puzzle catalogued by Erich Friedman, and records still fall: H. Lin's entries for 11 and 12 cubes in 3D date from July 2026. In four dimensions we found no published table.

Small cases are easy. Side 2 holds 16 cubes in a 2×2×2×2 grid, and side 3 holds 81 in a 3×3×3×3 grid. The interesting values lie between, where cubes have to tilt.

Constructions from lower dimensions

Known 2D and 3D packings combine into 4D ones. The best packing of 5 squares has side 2 + 1/√2 ≈ 2.7071: four squares in the corners and one turned 45° in the middle. Use that pattern in coordinates (x₁, x₂) and again in (x₃, x₄), and each pair of positions holds a 4-cube, giving 5 × 5 = 25 cubes at side 2.7071. A 3D packing can also be stacked in two layers along the fourth axis. Friedman's 13-cube record of side 2.956 gives 26 cubes, and his 14-cube record of side 2 + 7√2/10 ≈ 2.98995 gives 28.

That leaves a jump after 25 cubes: from 2.7071 straight to 2.956 for 26 and to 2.98995 for 27. Both new packings land inside that gap.

The packings

Both keep the skeleton of the 5 × 5 construction. Sixteen cubes sit nearly axis-aligned in the corners of the box. The rest occupy the channels between them, each turned by 25°–45° within one coordinate plane, and one is turned in two planes at once, the 4D analogue of the 45° centre square. Compared with the 25-cube construction, the corner frame opens up by about 0.24, which makes room for one or two more channel cubes.

The two sides agree with 2 + 2√2/3 to within 4 × 10⁻¹⁰ (n = 26) and 2 × 10⁻⁷ (n = 27). That points to a rigid structure with an exact description, but we have not derived it, so treat the closed form as a conjecture. Since 27 cubes fit, 26 do as well; the dedicated 26-cube run is slightly tighter.

How they were found

  1. Soft-to-rigid search. We adapted Yohei Nakajima's soft-to-rigid method to any dimension: particles start as balls and harden into cubes while the box shrinks. Each pair of cubes carries its own separating direction as a free variable, so a positive gap along that direction proves the pair does not overlap.
  2. Constrained polish. An augmented Lagrangian with L-BFGS minimizes the box side subject to pair, wall and orthogonality constraints, with hand-written gradients in C++. On the 5-square test it reaches 2 + 1/√2 to 10⁻¹⁰ in a third of a second. The search on this page is a line-for-line JavaScript port.
  3. Seeding from the construction. Random starts never beat the constructions in 4D. Starting from the 25-cube product spread to side 2.9, with the extra cubes dropped in at random, gave 2.943189 for n = 26 in the first batch of 20 seeds and 2.951791 for n = 27.
  4. Basin hopping. Twenty threads ran for ten minutes per n, each repeatedly turning, moving or swapping one or two cubes, re-polishing, and keeping improvements. That brought both to 2.942809.

Verification so far

An independent checker in NumPy tests every pair of cubes against all 56 candidate separating directions of the 4D separating-axis theorem, then checks the walls and the orthogonality of each rotation. Smallest gap between two cubes: 4.5 × 10⁻¹¹ (n = 26) and 1.05 × 10⁻¹⁰ (n = 27). Walls are exact to rounding and rotations orthogonal to 4 × 10⁻¹⁶.

What is still missing

  • An exact certificate in rational arithmetic with a safety margin, as Nakajima produced for his 3D claim. The current check uses double-precision floating point.
  • A thorough literature search. The 2.956 and 2.98995 baselines are our own constructions from 3D results, not published 4D values. We found no 4D catalogue, which does not rule one out.
  • A derivation of the conjectured exact side 2 + 2√2/3.

Coordinates

Each cube is a centre c ∈ ℝ⁴ and a 4×4 orthogonal matrix R whose columns are the cube's edge directions. Its vertices are c + R·(±½, ±½, ±½, ±½), and the box is [0, s]⁴. Centres are listed cube by cube; R is row-major, 16 numbers per cube.
